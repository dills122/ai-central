# Prose Pattern Catalog

Use this catalog to find clusters. A single occurrence is rarely a reason to rewrite.

## Content and claims

1. **Significance inflation:** ordinary facts framed as pivotal, enduring, symbolic, or part of a grand trend.
2. **Notability name-dropping:** source or media lists used as a substitute for relevant context.
3. **Superficial participle analysis:** trailing `-ing` phrases that gesture at meaning without explaining it.
4. **Promotional language:** copy such as `vibrant`, `breathtaking`, `renowned`, `seamless`, or `groundbreaking` without evidence.
5. **Vague attribution:** `experts`, `observers`, or `industry reports` where a source should be named or the claim removed.
6. **Formulaic challenge sections:** generic obstacles followed by an unsupported optimistic outlook.

## Language and grammar

7. **Stock AI vocabulary:** repeated abstract words such as `crucial`, `delve`, `landscape`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry`, or `underscore`.
8. **Copula avoidance:** inflated substitutes for `is`, `are`, or `has`, such as `serves as`, `stands as`, or `boasts`.
9. **Negative parallelism:** repeated `not just X but Y`, `not X; Y`, or clipped tailing negations.
10. **Forced groups of three:** unrelated ideas arranged into triplets for rhythm rather than meaning.
11. **Synonym cycling:** changing the name of the same thing merely to avoid repetition.
12. **False ranges:** `from X to Y` where the endpoints do not form a meaningful scale.
13. **Actorless prose:** passive constructions or fragments that hide a useful subject. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.

## Style and formatting

14. **Punctuation tics:** repeated em dashes, en dashes, semicolons, parentheses, or double hyphens. Compare with the author's sample; do not ban punctuation mechanically.
15. **Boldface overuse:** routine concepts formatted for artificial emphasis.
16. **Inline-header lists:** every bullet begins with a bold label and colon even when ordinary prose or a table is clearer.
17. **Mechanical heading case:** headings use a uniform style that conflicts with the document or author.
18. **Decorative emoji:** icons used as filler or pseudo-structure rather than as part of the established voice.
19. **Quotation normalization:** changing straight or curly quotation marks without a real style requirement.

## Conversation leakage

20. **Chatbot artifacts:** `I hope this helps`, offers to continue, or meta-introductions pasted into the content.
21. **Knowledge-gap filler:** training-cutoff disclaimers, speculation, or stock privacy phrases used instead of a source or a clear omission.
22. **Sycophancy:** praise or agreement that does not advance the substance.

## Filler and persuasion

23. **Wordy setup:** `in order to`, `due to the fact that`, `at this point in time`, and similar padding.
24. **Stacked hedging:** multiple qualifiers where one accurate uncertainty marker is enough.
25. **Generic positive conclusion:** `the future looks bright` or another send-off unsupported by concrete plans.
26. **Uniform compound styling:** treating every compound as hyphenated regardless of syntax or the project's style guide.
27. **Authority tropes:** `the real question`, `at its core`, or `what really matters` used to make an ordinary statement sound profound.
28. **Signposting announcements:** `let's dive in`, `here's what you need to know`, or other announcements instead of beginning the content.
29. **Fragmented heading warm-up:** a heading followed by a sentence that only restates the heading.
30. **Diff-anchored prose:** evergreen documentation describes what changed rather than how the system works now. Keep change framing in changelogs and migrations.
31. **Manufactured punchlines:** repeated short fragments and section-ending slogans engineered for drama.
32. **Aphorism formulas:** `X is the language of Y` or similar portable profundity that replaces a concrete claim.
33. **Fake-candid openers:** `Honestly?`, `Real talk`, or `Here's the thing` used as theatrical setup rather than genuine voice.

## False-positive check

Do not treat polished grammar, mixed registers, formal vocabulary, a common transition, one emphatic sentence, an isolated em dash, a quotation, or correct formatting as proof of machine authorship. Look for repeated combinations that make the prose less clear or less recognizably the author's.
